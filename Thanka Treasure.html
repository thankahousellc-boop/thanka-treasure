The Craft

A practice older than memory.

Before a single line is drawn, the artist sits in meditation. The canvas is stretched on a wooden frame, primed with chalk and yak-skin glue, then burnished with a smooth river stone for three days.

Pigments are ground from lapis lazuli, malachite, cinnabar, and saffron — held to the canvas with herbal binders prepared by hand. Gold is hammered into leaves so thin a breath disturbs them.

A single Thangka may take six months. The largest, more than a year. When the eyes are finally opened, the painting is brought to a lama and consecrated in a private rite.

The hands behind the work

Three masters. One unbroken lineage.

We work with a small circle of artists — most trained from childhood under their fathers and grandfathers in the Karma Gadri and Menri traditions.

T

Tenzin Dorjee

Karma Gadri lineage · 32 years

Trained in Lhasa under his uncle, a senior monk-painter, Tenzin specializes in wrathful protectors and the Wheel of Life. He grinds his own malachite each morning before the sun rises over Boudhanath.

"The painting is finished only when I no longer recognize my hand in it."
P

Pema Lhamo

Menri lineage · 19 years

One of a small but growing circle of women masters. Pema is known for her delicate Green Tara, her landscapes touched with spring, and a quiet line that looks almost handwritten.

"I learned to paint before I learned to read. The brush taught me both."
N

Ngawang Sherpa

Karma Gadri lineage · 41 years

Senior master of our atelier and teacher to a dozen apprentices. His mandalas — geometric, mathematical, almost shimmering — are held in private collections from Tokyo to Zürich.

"A mandala is a map of the mind. To paint it is to walk through it."
