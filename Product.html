Tenzin Dorjee

A 19-year apprentice of master Karma Tashi, Tenzin trained from age twelve in the ancient Karma Gadri tradition — a school known for soft landscape, restrained palette, and breath-thin gold detailing. His Buddhas are recognized across the Himalayas for the gentle quietude of their gaze.

"I do not paint a Buddha. I uncover the one who was already waiting in the cloth."

This piece took 480 hours: forty-five days of preparing the canvas alone, ten weeks of pigment work, six days of gold-leaf burnishing, and three days for the final eye-opening — the moment the painting becomes alive.

Symbols within this painting

Every gesture, color, and ornament in a Thangka is a visualisation. Here are five to look for in the Shakyamuni you'll receive.

Bhumisparsha Mudra

Right hand touching earth — calling the ground itself to witness his awakening.

Lotus Throne

Eight-petaled — the Eightfold Path. Born of mud, blooming pure.

Golden Aureole

Concentric halos for body, speech, and mind — the three doors of practice.

Twin Bodhisattvas

Maitreya and Manjushri — wisdom and loving-kindness flanking the awakened one.

Hanging your Thangka

  • 1Choose a place above eye level — Thangkas are meant to be looked up to, not down upon.
  • 2Keep it from direct sunlight, fireplaces, and steam — mineral pigments age slowly in cool, dry rooms.
  • 3Hang from the silk loop at the top dowel; never from the canvas itself.
  • 4Roll inward (face on the inside) when storing. Always with the wooden dowel at the bottom.

A simple practice

  • 1Begin with three breaths — settle the body, soften the gaze.
  • 2Rest your eyes on the Buddha's face. Don't analyse. Just receive.
  • 3Notice his posture: still, upright, unhurried. Let your spine echo it.
  • 4Recite, if it feels right: Om Muni Muni Maha Muniye Soha. Seven times is enough.
